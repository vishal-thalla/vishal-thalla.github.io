About

I'm a software engineer who likes systems that run on their own: services that recover from failure, agents that act on real infrastructure, and bots that make decisions from data.

I work across two main languages: TypeScript for product and UI work (React, Node.js, Angular, Electron) and Python for APIs, data and ML (FastAPI, Django, PyTorch). I also write C# and .NET when a platform needs it. I care about the unglamorous parts that decide whether something survives production: event-driven design, idempotency, observability, clean deploys on Kubernetes, and tests.

What pulls me in lately is where LLMs and ML meet real systems: tools that LLMs can call safely, agentic workflows, and ML experiments evaluated honestly enough that a negative result still counts as a result. The projects below are where I try those ideas in the open.

Selected work

Featured · Open source · Python

cnn-trading-signals

A research-grade pipeline that turns market data into images and asks whether a CNN can find turning points better than classical models, without lying to itself about it.

OHLCV bars are converted into 15×15 indicator images: 15 technical indicators, each computed over 15 look-back periods. Turning points are labelled, and a PyTorch CNN is trained against scikit-learn baselines under purged walk-forward validation, so no training window ever leaks into the period it is scored on.

Predictions go through a cost-aware backtester (spread and commission, execution on the next bar) and are compared against a benchmark, because a model that wins before costs and loses after them is not a result.

A ground-up rebuild of a 2023–24 team project co-built with Sirshen Munsamy.

  1. 01IngestOHLCV bars
  2. 02Encode15×15 indicator images
  3. 03LabelTurning points
  4. 04TrainPyTorch CNN vs sklearn baselines
  5. 05ValidatePurged walk-forward
  6. 06BacktestSpread, commission, next-bar fills
  7. 07CompareAgainst benchmark

Results · 6 experiments, out-of-sample, after costs

  • Learnable labels: every model found turning points well above chance (0.42–0.57 macro-F1, where random is 0.33).
  • The CNN was not the best model: gradient boosting on the same 15×15 inputs beat it on F1 in all six runs.
  • No clear edge over chance: no model's Sharpe beat the 95th percentile of 200 random strategies with the same class mix.
  • Lower risk on SPY: the models matched buy & hold's Sharpe (0.72–0.81 against 0.80) while in the market only 40–60% of the time, with smaller drawdowns, but they made less money.

A negative result reached with a sound method: the evaluation harness is the deliverable. Full tables in the README.
